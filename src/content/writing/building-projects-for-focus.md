A focused tool usually wins by narrowing the surface area. Fewer screens, fewer modes, and fewer decisions make the work easier to resume, which matters more than novelty once the project stops being a prototype.

That is the same reason the site leans on dense lists and operational labels instead of decorative presentation. The page should answer “where am I?” and “what should I do next?” before it tries to impress anyone.

I keep thinking about [Pebbles](/projects/pebbles) when I revisit this topic because the same principle applies there: if the input surface is too wide, the tool starts feeling like a chore. If the defaults are tight, the app can stay small and still feel complete.

The notes pages are the clearest proof of the pattern. [Rails for quick thoughts](/writing/notes/rails-for-quick-thoughts) and [Disposable observations](/writing/notes/disposable-observations) both work because they stop trying to be full systems. They are quick enough to trust.

When a project is trying to help someone focus, the interface should behave like a calm desk, not a dashboard. That means the transitions, labels, and states should all reduce friction instead of advertising every possible capability at once.

Related reading outside the site includes [Getting Real](https://basecamp.com/gettingreal) and [The Non-Designer’s Design Book](https://www.peachpit.com/store/non-designers-design-book-the-9780133966152), both of which are good reminders that constraint is often a feature, not a limitation.
